The answers that live in one person's head

October 11, 2026

Nous Research, an AI lab, confirmed this month that it raised $90 million at a $1.5 billion valuation and launched Hermes for Businesses, a set of AI agents for business users. TechCrunch reports it lets companies deploy customized AI agents that handle multi-step work while keeping their data private and secure (TechCrunch). Those are the company's claims, and I have not tested the product.

Here is why I think it is worth your attention anyway. A lot of money is now going toward one idea: AI that works from what your own business knows. That idea is right. It is also the one that most small businesses are ready for, because the knowledge is already there. It is just stuck.

A common bottleneck: the answers live in one head

Every business runs on answers. How do we price this kind of job? What did we promise that customer? Why did we stop doing that? What is the process when this goes wrong?

In most small businesses, those answers live in three places: in one or two people's memory, in old emails and call recordings, and in documents nobody can find. So the same questions get asked over and over, usually to the owner, usually at a bad time.

You know you have this bottleneck when you are the answer machine. Your team does not need your judgment on most of those questions. They need access to what you already decided.

How to spot it

  • Do your best people get interrupted all day with questions they have already answered?
  • When someone leaves, do you lose things you never wrote down?
  • Do you listen to sales calls or read old proposals and think "we figured this out once"?
  • Do new hires take months to learn what a binder could have told them in a week?

Count the questions you answered this week that you have answered before. That number is the size of the bottleneck.

How it gets automated, and stays fixed

I built a product for exactly this, called ActionLayer. You upload what a business already has, such as sales calls, proposals, job notes, reports and procedures, and AI pulls out summaries, action items, risks, themes and follow-ups. The results go into a searchable place where your team can ask questions and get answers from your own material.

You do not need my product to use the idea. The pattern is what matters, and it has four parts:

  1. Start with what already exists. Do not begin by writing a manual. Begin with the calls, emails and documents you already have. The knowledge is in there.
  2. Keep it in your hands. Decide up front where the material lives, who can see it, and how you take it with you if you change tools. This is the same question the Nous announcement raises, and it is the right one to ask of any vendor, including me.
  3. Check the answers. AI can be wrong or out of date. A good setup shows where an answer came from, so a person can check it against the source.
  4. Keep it current. A knowledge base that is not updated becomes the thing people stop trusting. Build the habit of adding to it into the work, not into a cleanup project you will never do.

Notice what is not on the list: choosing the fanciest model. The big announcements are about the engine. For you, the fix lasts because of the plain parts: what goes in, who can see it, and who keeps it fresh.

Be careful with "AI agent"

The phrase gets used for everything from a chatbot to something that takes actions on your behalf. If you are thinking "I want to build an AI agent for my business," ask one question first: which repeat job will it take over, and what happens when it gets it wrong? If the answer is vague, you are buying a demo. If the answer is "it answers the same ten customer questions and sends anything unusual to me," you are buying a fix.

What I would do on Monday

Write down the ten questions you answered this week that you have answered before. Next to each, note where the answer lives today. If most of them live in your head, you have found your bottleneck, and the next step is getting those answers somewhere your team can reach.
