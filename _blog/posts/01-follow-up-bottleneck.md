## A common bottleneck: the follow-up that depends on you

Almost every owner I talk to has this one. A lead asks for a quote. A customer says "let me think about it." A vendor owes you a document. Someone has to follow up, and that someone is you, or a person on your team who is also doing four other things.

When it works, it works because someone remembered. When it fails, nobody notices, because a follow-up that never happened leaves no trace. The deal just goes quiet.

That is the signature of a bottleneck. The work depends on one person's memory and attention, and it breaks the week that person is busy, sick or on vacation.

## How to spot it

You do not need a consultant for this part. Ask yourself four questions:

- Is there a task you do every week that starts with the words "I need to remember to"?
- Do you keep a list in your head, in your inbox, or on a sticky note of people you owe a reply?
- When you are out for three days, what stops?
- Have you ever found out weeks later that something was never sent?

If you answered yes to any of these, you have found a candidate. The fastest check is to look at your sent folder from last week and count how many messages were really the same message with a different name on it. Those are repeat work, and repeat work is what automation is for.

## How it gets automated, and stays fixed

Here is where most advice goes wrong. People hear "automate follow-ups" and buy a tool that sends emails on a timer. A month later the timer is sending the wrong message to the wrong people, nobody trusts it, and it gets switched off. The bottleneck is back, plus a subscription.

A fix that lasts has four parts:

1. **Write the rule in plain words first.** "When a quote goes out and there is no reply in three days, send a short check-in. If there is no reply after seven, tell me." If you cannot say it plainly, a machine cannot do it.
2. **Decide what a person still decides.** The routine part goes to the system. The judgment part, like a big customer or an awkward situation, comes back to you with the context attached.
3. **Make a miss visible.** The system should tell you when something did not go out. A silent failure is the exact thing you are trying to get rid of.
4. **Make sure someone owns it.** It should be built so that you, not a vendor and not a specialist you cannot reach, can change the rule when the business changes.

## What I would do on Monday

Pick the one follow-up that, if it vanished from your week, you would feel the most relief. Write down who it goes to, when, and what it says. Count how often it happens. That page is the start of a real fix, and it takes about fifteen minutes.
